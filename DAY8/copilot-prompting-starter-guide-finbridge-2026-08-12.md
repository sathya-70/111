## What is this guide for?

Microsoft 365 Copilot is now available across your everyday tools — Outlook, Word, and Excel. This guide gives you everything you need to start getting real value from it, today, without any technical knowledge.

The single most important thing to know: **what you ask Copilot, and how you ask it, makes all the difference.** This guide shows you how to ask well.

## The Good Prompt Formula

Use this simple structure every time. You don't need to memorise every word — just keep these four ingredients in mind:

```
ROLE + TASK + CONTEXT + FORMAT
```

| Ingredient | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Role** | Who should Copilot act as? | "As a professional email writer…" |
| **Task** | What do you want it to do? | "…draft a reply to this email…" |
| **Context** | What's the situation? | "…the client is asking about a delay in their case." |
| **Format** | How should the output look? | "…keep it under 100 words and use a friendly but professional tone." |

**Easy memory hook:** Think of it like briefing a new colleague. You wouldn't just say "write something" — you'd tell them what, why, and how you want it to land.

## Outlook — Email & Calendar

### Example Prompt 1 — Replying to a difficult message
> "Draft a professional reply to this email. The sender is frustrated that their query has not been responded to in five days. Acknowledge the delay, apologise briefly, and let them know I will follow up by end of week. Keep the tone calm and friendly, no more than three short paragraphs."

### Example Prompt 2 — Writing a meeting request
> "Write a meeting invite for a 30-minute catch-up with the finance team next Tuesday. The purpose is to review Q3 budget actuals. Include a short agenda: 1) review figures, 2) flag any variances, 3) agree next steps. Professional but not stiff."

### Example Prompt 3 — Summarising a long email thread
> "Summarise this email chain in five bullet points. Focus on what decisions have been made, what is still outstanding, and who is responsible for each action."

## Word — Documents & Reports

### Example Prompt 1 — First draft of a team update
> "Write a one-page team update for senior management. The topic is the rollout of the new expenses system. Cover: what we did this month, what went well, any blockers, and what is planned for next month. Use plain English, short paragraphs, and a professional tone."

### Example Prompt 2 — Improving an existing draft
> "Rewrite the paragraph below so it is clearer and easier to read. Remove jargon. Keep the meaning exactly the same but aim for no more than three sentences."
>
> *(Then paste in your paragraph.)*

### Example Prompt 3 — Creating a checklist or structured list
> "Turn the following notes into a clear, numbered checklist that a new starter could follow on their first day in the finance team. Make sure each step is an action, not a description."
>
> *(Then paste in your bullet notes.)*

## Excel — Data & Spreadsheets

### Example Prompt 1 — Understanding a formula someone else wrote
*Use this when a colleague has left you a spreadsheet full of formulas and you have no idea what they do. You don't need to understand the formula — just copy and paste it.*

> "There is a formula in my spreadsheet and I don't know what it does. Can you explain it in plain English, step by step, as if I have never used Excel before? Here it is: [paste the formula from your spreadsheet here]"

### Example Prompt 2 — Writing a formula from a description
> "I have a spreadsheet with staff names in column A, their department in column B, and their expenses total in column C. Write a formula that adds up all expenses for people in the 'Finance' department only."

### Example Prompt 3 — Summarising or sense-checking data
> "Look at this table of monthly spend figures. Tell me which three months had the highest spend, which had the lowest, and whether there are any figures that look unusual compared to the rest."

## A Word on Data Safety — Please Read

**The good news first:** Copilot inside your FinBridge Microsoft 365 account is approved for work use. Your conversations stay within FinBridge's systems — you are not sending anything to the public internet. Use it confidently for your everyday tasks.

**The one thing to watch:** Free AI tools you might use in a personal browser — such as ChatGPT or Google Gemini — are a different story. They are external services, and anything you type into them could potentially be stored or used outside your control.

So if you are ever tempted to use a free AI tool on your work laptop or phone, keep this rule in mind:

> **Do not paste client details, financial figures, or any work documents into a free, public AI tool.**

This is the same data-safety habit from Day 1 of this programme: treat a public AI tool like a public noticeboard. If you would not pin the information up in a coffee shop, do not paste it in.

Not sure whether a tool is approved? Just ask your line manager or the IT helpdesk — no question is too basic.

## Getting Better Results — Three Quick Tips

1. **More context = better output.** If Copilot gives you something off-target, add a sentence explaining who the audience is or what tone you need.
2. **Keep going, don't start over.** If the first draft isn't quite right, just tell Copilot what to fix — "Make it shorter", "Make it sound more formal", or "Add a sentence about X". You can go back and forth as many times as you like. Think of it as a conversation, not a one-shot request.
3. **Always review before you send or save.** Copilot is a first-draft tool. You are accountable for what goes out under your name. Read it, check it, then use it.
